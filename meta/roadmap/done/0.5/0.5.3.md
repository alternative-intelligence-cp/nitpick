# 0.5.3 — `move`, moved-from bindings, and manual memory

**Cycle 0.5 — Static analyses.**

## Goal

A moved-from binding is invalid until reinitialised (D-065).

## The rule

`move(place)` is a **keyword operator on a place**, not a qualifier and not a
function — it returns no `Result` and could not be one. D-065's own example is the
test:

```nitpick
wild int8->:buffer = malloc(100i64);
wild int8->:moved  = move(buffer);

free(buffer);   // use after move
```

**Explicit only, no implicit moves.** Nothing moves because it was assigned or
passed; a move happens where `move` is written, which is what makes the analysis a
question about one operator rather than about every expression.

## Work

1. Moved-from state per binding, merged at branches the way assignment is —
   moved on one arm and not the other is **moved**, which is the opposite
   direction from definite assignment and is the easiest thing here to get
   backwards.
2. Reinitialisation clears it: a binding assigned after a move is valid again.
3. `nodrop` — the qualifier parses and nothing reads it. It suppresses the drop
   of a `wild` binding, which is the one case where *not* dropping is correct
   rather than a leak.
4. **The qualifiers get their meaning**: `wild` and `wildx` mark unchecked manual
   memory, and this is where "which allocations are live" starts being tracked at
   all.

## What belongs to Phase B and is said here so it is not looked for

The **K-semantics `exit` rule** — no live `wild`/`wildx` at `exit`, or it traps
back into `failsafe` — needs a runtime allocation registry
(`SAFETY_ARCHITECTURE.md`: "memory allocations must be tracked globally"). That is
Phase B's, and the static half here is only the part visible in one function.

Saying so is the point: a leak check that looked complete and covered one function
would be a promise nothing keeps.

## Watch for

**The merge runs the opposite way from definite assignment.** Assigned-on-some-path
is *not assigned*; moved-on-some-path *is* moved. Both are the conservative answer
and they point in different directions, which is exactly the kind of asymmetry a
shared merge helper gets wrong once and silently.

## Done when

- Using a binding after `move` is refused, and D-065's own example is the test.
- Reassigning after a move makes it usable again.
- Moved on one branch and not the other is refused after the merge.

---

## What this found

**A free is a move.** `dalloc(p)` transfers ownership out of `p` exactly as
`move(p)` does, so use-after-free and double-free fall out of the moved-from
machinery with no new state at all. D-065 says as much — `move` is "the mechanism
that prevents double-free on `wild` allocations" — and taking it literally is what
made item 4 ("which allocations are live") cheap rather than a subcycle of its own.

**Nothing in the real frontend checked that an operand had an address.** `@`,
`$$i`, `$$m` and `move` were unchecked entirely, and the assignment target's check
looked at the outermost operator only — so `f().x = 5i32;` passed as "a member
access", a store into a field of a value with no home. The SEED refuses all of
these, out of necessity, so the two compilers disagreed about what a valid program
is; that is the same situation `fixed` was in before 0.5.2, found the same way.

Recorded as **D-119**.

## Two things in the source material that do not work

- **D-065's own example does not compile.** It is written with a variable named
  `buffer`, and `buffer` is a keyword. The plan says "D-065's own example is the
  test", so the test is that example with the name changed and a note saying why.
- **`BUILTIN_REFERENCE` claimed `free` and `realloc` were "legacy aliases", and they
  never were.** Checked against the prototype rather than guessed: its type checker
  knows exactly `alloc`, `calloc`, `ralloc` and `dalloc`, and every `malloc` in the
  specs and in its parser tests sits inside `extern "libc"`. The line dates from the
  era when the compiler was C/C++-backed and `malloc`/`free` were what there was;
  the native allocator that replaced them never had aliases. Line removed, and the
  ownership check now knows two builtin names rather than four.

  The prototype also decided "is this a deallocator" by **matching the spelling** —
  a set containing `close`, `release`, `destroy` and `cleanup`, plus any name ending
  in `_free`, `_close`, `_destroy` or `_release`. That part is deliberately not
  carried over: `window_close` acquiring ownership semantics it never declared is
  the context-dependence the blueprint philosophy refuses.

## What a parameter still cannot say

`wild int8->:p` is a **parse error**: `p_parse_param` accepts `limit<Rules>` and
`_~` and no qualifier at all. So the manual-memory regime cannot be stated in a
signature, which is the same hole 0.5.1 found for `borrow_imm` / `borrow_mut` and
settled by not needing it (D-117). It is defensible here for the same shape of
reason — a parameter's allocation regime is the caller's business — and it is
written down because the next person to try it will hit the parse error and not
know whether it is a gap or a decision.
